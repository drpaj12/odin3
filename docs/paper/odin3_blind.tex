\title{AnonSynth, the Final Frontier:\\
Verilog Synthesis for FPGAs\\Built by the DarkFactory and Leads to AnonSuite}

\author{\IEEEauthorblockN{Anonymous Author(s)}
\IEEEauthorblockA{Affiliation withheld for double-blind review}}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Odin was written in 2005 and Odin~II in 2010. Odin~II became the Verilog front end of the VTR FPGA CAD flow, and Yosys with the Parmys plugin has since displaced it. After the moment of December 2025 when AI coding agents began to make real software, we asked the final-frontier question: could a Dark Factory, in which agents turn a human's specification into software, build AnonSynth, matching Odin~II's good ideas and plugging into the VTR flow? AnonSynth (MIT license) is a C17 framework with one provenance-tracked IR, two-stage elaboration, re-implementations of Yosys~0.55's \code{proc}, \code{opt}, \code{fsm}, \code{wreduce} and \code{flatten}, architecture-driven partial mapping, linked ABC and VPR-ready BLIF, a SystemVerilog front end over the slang library, and a versioned C ABI for plugins and AI tools. Since v1.0 it has also gained circuit properties (graphlet orbit counts and SCOAP testability), a netlist editing API, testbench simulation, and the ability to read and simulate processor designs (v1.3.0). Each claim of the paper is a hypothesis with an experiment that confirms it or not. Possibly good: every netlist AnonSynth produced for the 15 VTR benchmarks that fit the development laptop routes in VPR, with geomean CLB, channel-width, delay and wirelength ratios against the Parmys flow inside a 1.20 gate, but two designs differ from Parmys in multiplier count and five lose adders, which the gate's equal-or-explained rule does not allow. Confirmed: refinement proofs, functional checks and byte-identical reruns hold; checking against oracles found defects in the Parmys flow; and, when enabled (\code{--prov-ast}, off by default on the command line), provenance records the chain of HDL constructs behind every object. Not confirmed: recording constructs at elaboration costs more than 5\% of read time on two of five large designs, so it is off by default; equivalence on the 14 small VTR designs is only partly proved. The C ABI is stated, not tested. Power is not measured. We state the caveats of this build: a human watched every day, the code has not been deeply read by a human, and testing covers a subset of scenarios. We report the time and token cost, and present the AnonSuite ecosystem that this approach made possible.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

\emph{The problem in plain English.}
An FPGA is a chip of programmable logic blocks and wires onto which a digital design is loaded. % def:fpga
Its storage cell is the flip-flop, which keeps one bit between clock edges, % def:ff
and its logic cell is the LUT (look-up table), a small memory that computes any function of six inputs and, with a flip-flop, forms the basic logic element. % def:lut
LUTs and flip-flops are grouped into CLBs (configurable logic blocks), which are placed as one unit~\cite{luuthesis}. % def:clb
A designer describes the circuit in a hardware description language (HDL), here Verilog, % def:hdl
and a tool turns that text into a netlist, the list of gates, LUTs and other cells and the wires between them. % def:netlist
The tool that does the first part of this job is the synthesis front end. % def:front
Its first step, elaboration, resolves parameters, generate loops and hierarchy and turns the parsed HDL into a design graph. % def:elab
ABC then maps the gates of the netlist onto LUTs~\cite{brayton2010abc}. % def:abc
After that, packing groups the LUTs and flip-flops into CLBs, placement chooses a site on the chip for each CLB, and routing chooses the wires between them. % def:prp
The open-source flow that does all of this is VTR (Verilog-to-Routing), and VPR is its packing, placement and routing program~\cite{anon11,luuthesis}. % def:vtr
VPR reads the netlist as BLIF, the Berkeley Logic Interchange Format~\cite{blif}. % def:blif
A front end that gives VTR good results is not enough for the research we want to do. % why
Synthesis changes the designer's structure: it flattens hierarchy, merges and splits operators, and covers logic with LUTs that no source line names. % why2
The link from each object of the final netlist back to the HDL that produced it is its provenance, % def:prov
and no front end that serves VTR keeps it.

\emph{Why AnonSynth.}
Odin~\cite{anon09} started from nothing. It used Icarus Verilog~\cite{icarus} as the front end, but Icarus treats Verilog as a simulation language, and building synthesis on a simulator's front end cost a year of work to learn the basics of Verilog synthesis. Odin~II~\cite{anon10} followed and wrote a proper compiler front end: a Bison and Flex parser, and elaboration into a flattened netlist. Odin~II became the Verilog front end of the VTR flow~\cite{anon11,luu2014vtr7}. VTR has since moved its default front end to Parmys~\cite{parmys}, an extension of Yosys~\cite{wolf2013yosys,yosys} that carries Odin~II's mapping of multipliers, memories and adders onto dedicated blocks, and the VTR documentation marks Odin~II as deprecated~\cite{vtrodindoc}. % def:odin2

Then came the moment of December 2025: AI coding agents~\cite{claudecode} became able to make real software. Yu and Ren's multi-agent self-evolved ABC~\cite{yu2026selfevolvedabc} showed that LLM agents can change the source of a million-line logic synthesis system, rebuild it, check it, and keep the changes that improve its results; VPR-Evolve~\cite{wu2026vprevolve} has since done the same for FPGA place and route, and Ding et al.\ survey high-level synthesis in the agentic era~\cite{ding2026hlsagentic}. Together they raised the final-frontier question. Dan Shapiro describes five levels of AI-assisted programming; at the last, the Dark Factory, the software process is ``a black box that turns specs into software''~\cite{shapiro2026darkfactory}. % def:dark
Could we use a Dark Factory to build AnonSynth, a front end that matches Odin~II's good ideas and plugs into the VTR flow where Yosys has displaced Odin~II? This paper describes the answer to that question (YES!), what has been built to answer it, and how it is tested. AnonSynth is public (URL withheld for review).

\emph{What counts as a good result.}
A front end is judged on several measures at once, and the dataset we want from it must carry all of them.
The routing channel width is the number of wire tracks per channel that the router needs, written $W$; % def:minw
the wirelength is the total length of wire the routed design uses; % def:wl
the critical path is the slowest path between registers, and its delay is the critical-path delay (CPD); % def:cpd
a near-critical path is one whose delay is within a stated window of the CPD. % def:near
Area is how much chip a design uses: its LUTs, its CLBs and $W$, and a good result keeps it low. % def:area
Speed is how fast the circuit runs: the CPD and the wirelength, and where available the near-critical paths, and a good result keeps it high, on the critical and on the near-critical paths alike. % def:speed
Power is the energy the circuit uses, and a good result keeps it low; this paper does not measure power. % def:power
The reason for the provenance link is the AnonData dataset: a record that shows \emph{why} each structure was placed where it was, through the HDL construct it came from, for all of these measures at once.

\emph{Claims and experiments.}
Each claim in this paper is a hypothesis, a statement that an experiment can confirm or fail to confirm. % def:hyp
The experiment gives a verdict: USEFUL (confirmed under the stated gate or check), POSSIBLY GOOD (confirmed but narrow or fragile, so stated with the fragility and never as proof alone), INCONCLUSIVE (neither confirmed nor failed), or DISMISSED (failed). Each hypothesis has a label, \emph{O1} to \emph{O17} (O for AnonSynth), taken from the research log. Sec.~\ref{sec:results} gives each hypothesis one argument: the hypothesis, its implication, the experiment, the methodology, the measurements, their interpretation and the conclusion with the verdict. A failed one is in the Discussion and the research log.

\emph{The innovations in plain English.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{A front end that plugs into VTR.} A program that reads Verilog and hands a netlist to VTR, so ABC, packing, placement and routing work as before and the result is about as good as with the Yosys-based front end, comparable and not equal.
\item \textbf{Checking against oracles.} Every stage is compared with a trusted reference tool, an oracle, % def:oracle
and where the two disagree a probe decides which one matches the Verilog, so the reference's own mistakes surface.
\item \textbf{One provenance-tracked IR.} Every object remembers which Verilog text made it, through every pass, in one internal design graph, the IR (intermediate representation), % def:ir
and from v1.2.0 it remembers the whole chain of constructs, including the places where two constructs were blended.
\item \textbf{A versioned C ABI.} A stable interface (the ABI) so a plugin, a separately built program that adds features, and AI tools can drive and read AnonSynth without changing it. % def:abi
\end{enumerate}
The way AnonSynth was built, with a Dark Factory, is the method and context of the paper and not a tested innovation (Sec.~\ref{sec:method-built}).

\emph{Contributions.} The contributions are the innovations with the hypotheses that confirmed them:
\begin{itemize}
\item the front end and its flow, comparable to Parmys with two caveats (O1, POSSIBLY GOOD; O2, USEFUL; Sec.~\ref{sec:q-qor});
\item checking against oracles: proofs that the lowering pass keeps the design's behaviour, a functional check, identical output on rerun, a corpus run and six defects found in the Parmys flow (O4, O6, O7, O8, O9, O10, all USEFUL; Secs.~\ref{sec:q-proof} to \ref{sec:parmys-bugs});
\item provenance: the construct chain and joins, exports that read back and a neutral timing attribution (O14, O15, USEFUL; O17, POSSIBLY GOOD); the cost of recording constructs failed its gate (O16, DISMISSED; Secs.~\ref{sec:q-prov} to \ref{sec:q-timing});
\item the platform features added since v1.0: graphlet and SCOAP properties, netlist editing, testbench simulation and processor netlists (Sec.~\ref{sec:platform}; evidence in the tool's test suite and not a hypothesis of this paper);
\item an account of the build with a Dark Factory under daily human oversight, with its time and token cost and its limits (Sec.~\ref{sec:method-built});
\item a presentation of AnonSuite, the suite this approach made possible, including a reimplementation of an earlier Verilog teaching game~\cite{anon01}, now called AnonGame.
\end{itemize}

The approach has limits, and we state them first. It was not a lights-out factory: we wrote the design specification and goals, ruled on every design rule, and watched the agents daily. Everything ran on one Windows desktop (Intel i7-13700) in a WSL2 virtual machine with 12 logical processors and 23\,GiB of memory, where an out-of-memory condition kills the machine and every agent in it, so heavy jobs run one at a time under a lock and the largest VTR designs were not run. The codebase has not been deeply read by a human. It took 14 calendar days to reach version~1.0 (2026-10-08 to 2026-10-21) and was tested with a subset of the possible scenarios. We found defects in the Parmys flow (Sec.~\ref{sec:parmys-bugs}), which we will report to those projects in good faith.

We call this new suite AnonSuite. AnonSynth is its trunk: every path through the suite passes its one provenance IR. The other tools of the suite (an experiment runner and GUI, a simulation bridge, a game, a reverse-engineering tool, a placement experiment, the circuit knowledge base and a record-and-replay viewer) branch from it and use it as a package. All the code is public (URL withheld for review).


\section{Background}
\label{sec:background}

This section introduces, in the order in which the experiments need them, the ideas behind AnonSynth: who introduced each, and which experiment of Sec.~\ref{sec:results} uses it. The basic FPGA and CAD terms of the Introduction follow the thesis on heterogeneous FPGAs and the thesis on architecture-aware packing and VTR~\cite{luuthesis}.

\subsection{Two open-source models of Verilog synthesis}

\emph{The Odin~II flow.} Odin~II~\cite{anon10} parses Verilog with Bison and Flex into an \emph{abstract syntax tree} (AST), the tree of declarations, statements and expressions of the HDL text with their source locations~\cite{ast}, % def:ast
and turns it into one flattened netlist of nodes, pins, and nets. It reads the VPR architecture file to decide which multipliers, adders, and memories become dedicated blocks, and writes the rest as BLIF for ABC~\cite{brayton2010abc}.

\emph{The Yosys approach.} Yosys~\cite{wolf2013yosys} parses Verilog into an AST, and \code{genrtlil} turns each module into RTLIL, a netlist of wires, cells, processes and memories with the hierarchy kept. A \emph{word cell} is a multi-bit operator, a \emph{bit cell} is a one-bit gate, and the pass \code{lower} turns word cells into bit cells (in Yosys, \code{techmap} does this). % def:word
Every later step is a pass over RTLIL that never sees the AST. AnonSynth re-implements the word-level passes of Yosys~0.55: \code{proc} turns processes into multiplexers and flip-flops, \code{opt} cleans up, \code{fsm} recodes state machines, \code{wreduce} narrows widths and \code{flatten} inlines the hierarchy. % def:passes
Yosys also reaches SystemVerilog and VHDL through plugins~\cite{yosysslang,ghdl}. AnonSynth takes the RTLIL route, and experiments O4 and O10 test its \code{lower}.

\subsection{VTR and dedicated blocks}

Both models attach to VTR~\cite{anon12,anon11,luu2014vtr7,murray2020vtr8}: a front end, ABC, then VPR's packing, placement, and routing on an XML-described architecture. \emph{Partial mapping} is binding the parts of a design that fit the dedicated blocks of the architecture (multipliers, adders, memories) to those blocks and leaving the rest as gates for ABC; Odin~II is where it began~\cite{anon10}. % def:pmap
Jason Luu's architecture description language~\cite{luu2011packing} models each dedicated block's primitive, so a front end that reads the same file can do the binding; VTR~7.0~\cite{luu2014vtr7} and the study of hard adders~\cite{luu2014hardadders} carried this to adders. Parmys~\cite{parmys} brings it onto Yosys's word-level netlists and is now VTR's default front end. Experiments O1, O11 and O12 compare AnonSynth's partial mapping with Parmys's.

\subsection{The provenance problem}
\label{sec:provenance-problem}

Synthesis modifies the designer's structure: flattening removes the hierarchy, optimization merges and splits operators, and LUT mapping covers the logic with cells that no source line names. We call the task of linking each object of the final netlist back to the HDL that produced it the \emph{provenance problem}. Earlier work addressed it in placement by recovering system-level information from the flat netlist and analyzing its correlation with locality~\cite{anon04,anon05}, then identified and placed clusters found in the placement data~\cite{anon06}; later work applies data-mining clustering to such algorithms~\cite{anon07}. Goeders and Wilton trace signals of a high-level synthesis-built circuit back to their source for in-system debug~\cite{goeders2014effective,goeders2015dynamic,goeders2017signaltracing}. Each had to recover a link the synthesis tool didn't keep. AnonSynth keeps it in a \emph{lineage DAG}: the append-only graph of source, imported and derived records, with labelled joins, behind each provenance ID (Sec.~\ref{sec:arch}). % def:lineage
Its analysis flow can redo the placement-correlation experiment with the link to the HDL measured rather than recovered. Experiments O14 to O17 test the lineage DAG, its exports, its cost and its use for timing.

\subsection{Oracles and checks}

An oracle makes the process much simpler than the original creation of earlier synthesis tools, which we verified with humans, at high cost. \code{tools/run-oracle.sh} stores an oracle's netlist, logs and commit as a \emph{golden}: the stored result that later runs compare against. % def:golden
An oracle is trusted because it is the reference, not because it is always right (Sec.~\ref{sec:parmys-bugs}). The comparison has four forms. \emph{Equivalence} is a SAT proof that two designs give the same outputs (\emph{refinement}: one design's behaviours are among the other's); a design past the size limit of the solver stays unproven. % def:equiv
Equivalence checking is built into ABC~\cite{mishchenko2005fraigs,brayton2010abc}, whose combinational check experiment O8 uses. The \emph{functional check} (\code{fcheck}) is VTR's random check: build the RTL and the netlist with Verilator~\cite{verilator} and drive both with 50,000 random vectors. % def:fcheck
\emph{Determinism} means that rerunning gives byte-identical output. % def:det
The \emph{exit-code discipline} is our convention that a list of expected differences never turns an unproven result into a pass: tools report 0 holds, 1 differs, 2 tool error, 3 hard failure, 4 unproven, 77 skipped. % def:exit
Experiments O4, O5 and O10 use equivalence, O6 the functional check, O7 determinism and O9 the oracle comparison itself.

\subsection{Measuring quality of results}

VTR measures a flow by running designs through ABC, packing, placement and routing and reading the area and speed measures of the Introduction from VPR~\cite{anon11,luu2014vtr7,murray2020vtr8}. We summarise a run over many designs by the \emph{geomean ratio}, the geometric mean over designs of the ratio AnonSynth over Parmys: 1.0 means equal and below 1.0 is better for area and delay. % def:geomean
A \emph{leg} is one run of the quality-of-results harness \code{vtr-qor}: leg~P is the Parmys flow (the baseline), leg~O is AnonSynth with its linked ABC, and leg~O$'$ is AnonSynth's netlist through VTR's own ABC stage. % def:leg
The \emph{QoR gate} is our acceptance bound, and the authors' choice: geomean ratio at most 1.20 and no design above 1.50 on the gated metrics. It says comparable, not equal. % def:gate
Experiments O1 and O2 use it.

\section{The Innovations}
\label{sec:arch}

Each subsection gives the innovation in plain English first and the technical description second. The experiments that test them are in Sec.~\ref{sec:results}.

\subsection{A front end that plugs into VTR}
\label{sec:vtr-link}

\emph{Plain English.} AnonSynth reads Verilog, makes a netlist, and hands it to ABC and VPR as before. Plugging into VTR is not the real goal of AnonSynth; it is the proof that each step works on real designs, because VTR comes with golden results to compare with. The result is meant to be comparable with the Yosys-based front end, not equal to it.

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centering
\odinfit{\textwidth}{\input{IMAGE/blind/fig_flow}}
\caption{The flow of a Verilog design through AnonSynth, in the order of the production script. Each box is a stage; the text under it says what it does. Every stage from \code{proc} to \code{write\_blif} is a registered pass.}
\label{fig:flow}
\end{figure*}

\emph{Technical.} AnonSynth reads a design into one IR and changes it one pass at a time (Fig.~\ref{fig:flow}). Elaboration runs in two stages and the word-level passes follow Yosys~0.55; \code{partial\_map} binds multipliers, adders and RAMs to the blocks the architecture lists, with Parmys's rule as the default; \code{lower} turns word cells into bit cells; a linked ABC maps the logic to LUTs; and \code{write\_blif --vtr} writes the BLIF that VPR reads. Where AnonSynth and Parmys could differ, \code{--vtr-compat} makes AnonSynth do what Parmys does and print a WARNING; where Parmys is wrong to the RTL, AnonSynth stays correct and declares the difference (Sec.~\ref{sec:parmys-bugs}). Yosys is the more complete and mature tool by a wide margin; the differences from Odin~II and Yosys are choices made for research goals, not claims of superiority.

\subsection{Checking against oracles}

\emph{Plain English.} Every stage of AnonSynth is checked by a tool that is separate from the code, against a reference. Where AnonSynth and the reference differ, a probe decides which one matches the Verilog, so the reference's own mistakes surface, and an unproven result never counts as a pass.

\emph{Technical.} The techniques are refinement proofs by SAT (\code{lower-equiv}, \code{opt-equiv}), random module generators checked against Yosys \code{techmap}, Verilator random vectors (\code{fcheck}), golden BLIFs, and the exit-code convention of Sec.~\ref{sec:abi-summary}.

\subsection{One provenance-tracked IR}

\emph{Plain English.} Every object in the netlist remembers which Verilog text made it, and from v1.2.0 it remembers the full chain of constructs, including the places where two constructs were blended. This is AnonSynth's answer to the provenance problem (Sec.~\ref{sec:provenance-problem}) and the property the AnonData dataset rests on.

\emph{Technical.} Every node, net and wire carries one 32-bit ID into an append-only lineage DAG. A record is SOURCE (made from the HDL), IMPORTED (read from a netlist) or DERIVED (made by a pass, with its parents), and it holds the pass run and the operation within it, so 100 adders from one line of a generate loop stay distinguishable. A step that blends constructs is labelled a join. The walk runs backward to the source line and forward from \code{file:line}, and the writers can name each object \code{hier/path/name@file:line}, so a VPR block traces to its source line without AnonSynth loaded. v1.2.0 adds the construct table (ABI~7) and a version~2 name map.

\subsection{The ABI: extensions and AI tools}
\label{sec:abi-summary}

\emph{Plain English.} Other programs of the suite can drive and read AnonSynth through one stable interface, and AI tools attach the same way.

\emph{Technical.} The interface is the contract between compiled code: function names, types, structure layouts and calling conventions. A plugin built against one version must load against a later build without a recompile, and a C ABI helps: no name mangling, no class layouts, no exceptions across the boundary, and other languages can call it. \code{include/anonsynth/anonsynth.h} is the only header a plugin sees. The ABI version (7 since v1.2.0 and still 7 at v1.3.0, whose changes are additive exports) exposes an opaque design handle and raw 32-bit IDs; every function returns a status, \code{libanonsynth.so} exports only what the header declares, and a plugin that asks for another ABI version is refused. Plugins register passes and cell types from an \code{anonsynth\_plugin\_init} entry point, and Python uses the same header through cffi.

The same boundary is how AI tools attach. Agents built AnonSynth by driving its command-line scripts and reading its tools' exit codes (0 holds, 1 differs, 2 tool error, 3 hard failure, 4 unproven, 77 skipped), and through the Python binding an agent can walk the IR and its provenance and add cell types, recording its decisions as DERIVED provenance records. No hypothesis tests the ABI itself here; the suite papers use it.

\subsection{The platform features of v1.3.0}
\label{sec:platform}

\emph{Plain English.} After v1.0, the research tools built on AnonSynth asked for four things: measure a netlist, change it, run it against a testbench, and read real processors. AnonSynth now offers all four, checked by the tool's own test suite and not as hypotheses of this paper. \emph{Circuit properties:} graphlet orbit counts (2 to 5 nodes undirected, 2 to 4 directed; our own counter, equal to the ORCA test oracle on 473 netlists) and SCOAP testability, each keyed to provenance. \emph{Netlist editing:} nodes, nets and pins can be added, rewired and removed through the C ABI and Python, and the edit events are exported. \emph{Testbench simulation:} \code{sim\_tb} runs a Verilog testbench subset against the RTL or the written netlist. \emph{Processor netlists:} the Arduino-on-RISC-V microcontroller and the DarkRISCV system on chip run under \code{sim\_tb} and match iverilog. Where AnonSynth follows Parmys or Yosys: hierarchical references into another module are undriven nets with a WARNING, and a sole-driver \code{\$tribuf} loses its enable.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology}

\subsection{Setup, designs and metrics}

The benchmarks are the VTR~7.0 designs on \code{k6\_frac\_N10\_frac\_chain\_mem32K\_40nm}, run through VTR commit \code{3c9a4d23}, whose Yosys~0.55 and Parmys are the baseline. The local set is the 15 designs that fit the development laptop; the largest five (\code{mcml}, \code{LU32PEEng}, \code{bgm}, \code{LU8PEEng} and \code{LU64PEEng}) were left out because of our machine and nothing is claimed for them. Smaller micro-benchmarks (68 to 539 designs, per experiment) test single passes. Each design is run once unless the experiment says otherwise. \emph{M2} names the table of the QoR harness for the 15 designs after the fix of lost RAMs (Sec.~\ref{sec:q-qor}).

Of the three measures of a good result (Sec.~\ref{sec:intro}) the paper measures two, both read from VPR: area (LUTs, CLBs and $W$) and speed (the CPD and the routed wirelength). No experiment measures near-critical paths (Sec.~\ref{sec:future}), and power is not measured.

\subsection{Gates, checks and statistics}

AnonSynth is a systems paper: its evidence is gates and checks against oracles, with no p-value anywhere. Each experiment states its gate or check: the QoR gate for quality of results; a proof outcome (ok, expected difference, unexpected difference, unproven) for equivalence; a count of passing designs for functional checks; byte equality for identical output; a time and memory ratio for cost. Where no number decides the question the verdict is INCONCLUSIVE, and a result never gets a stronger verdict than its numbers.

\subsection{How it was built: a Dark Factory}
\label{sec:method-built}

How AnonSynth was built is the method and context of the paper, not a hypothesis tested in it: there is one build and no comparison arm, so it shows that this process can reach v1.0 under these conditions and not that it generalises. Every sub-project followed one path: a specification with numbered decisions, a critique by a fresh read-only agent, a plan, tasks for implementer agents with disjoint files, a review of every task before a pull request opens, and a merge only on a green CI gate. A controller agent owns git. We approve the specifications and design rules; elsewhere the agents proceed on their own recommendations, logged as defaults we may override. Exit tests are external, against oracles (Sec.~\ref{sec:results}).


Cost is logged from the agents' transcripts. To the v1.0 close, 317.6\,h of wall-clock time, 211.4\,h of it active, 63,540 requests used 15.1 billion tokens (95\% prompt-cache reads, 65.8 million output), across 16 main sessions and 880 subagent runs; 226 pull requests merged. Subagents used 82\% of the tokens. Our own part was about 400 messages (about 9,400 words): the specification, the decisions, and the reviews. The work ran on a Claude Max subscription.

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:results}


Each result is one argument with seven parts, always in this order: the \emph{hypothesis} (what we claim), its \emph{implication} (why it matters if true), the \emph{experiment} (what is run, on what, against what), the \emph{methodology} (the measure and the gate), the \emph{measurements} (the numbers), their \emph{interpretation} (what the numbers mean) and the \emph{conclusion} with its verdict. The labels O1 to O17 are those of the full version of this paper, which lists every hypothesis, including those that are not confirmed. The arguments build on one another: first the baseline and the quality of results, then the checks of function, then the defects the checks found, then provenance.

\subsection{Quality of results against Parmys (O2, O1)}

\subsection{Refinement proof of lower (O4, O10)}
\label{sec:q-proof}
\label{sec:verify-evidence}

\argid{O4}
\ah{Hypothesis} \code{lower} is a refinement of the word-level design on the micro-benchmarks.
\ah{Implication} If true, the step that turns word cells into bit cells never changes what a design does, so later quality differences are not caused by it.
\ah{Experiment} The refinement proof \code{lower-equiv} on 272 micro designs, and a sweep over 526 fixtures.
\ah{Methodology} A proof outcome per design: ok, expected difference, unexpected difference or unproven (past the size limit of the SAT solver).
\ah{Measurements} 399 ok, 140 expected and 0 unexpected results. The sweep compared 518 fixtures and found no difference; the other 8 are unproven at the size limit.
\ah{Interpretation} None of the 539 proof results shows an unexpected behaviour change; the expected differences are listed in advance.
\ah{Conclusion} \code{lower} keeps the design's behaviour on these micros. Verdict: USEFUL.

\argid{O10}
\ah{Hypothesis} \code{lower} agrees with Yosys~0.55's \code{techmap} on random modules.
\ah{Implication} If true, an independent implementation of the same step gives the same logic, which is evidence that neither is wrong in the same way.
\ah{Experiment} The generator of random Verilog modules \code{lower-gen-diff} feeds both tools; the outputs are compared.
\ah{Methodology} A structural comparison per seed, and an equivalence proof where the structures differ.
\ah{Measurements} 200 ok of 200 seeds. A 5,000-seed run gave 4,830 ok, 142 structural differences that are proved equivalent, 28 empty designs and no bug.
\ah{Interpretation} All 4,972 non-empty cases agree in logic: 4,830 are the same circuit and 142 differ in structure but are proved equivalent.
\ah{Conclusion} \code{lower} and \code{techmap} agree. Verdict: USEFUL.

\subsection{Function against the source (O6, O7, O8)}

\argid{O6}
\ah{Hypothesis} AnonSynth's netlists behave like the RTL.
\ah{Implication} If true, the netlists can be used in place of the source for any experiment that measures behaviour.
\ah{Experiment} \code{fcheck} builds the RTL and the netlist and drives both on the 15 designs.
\ah{Methodology} 50,000 random vectors per design; a design passes when all outputs agree.
\ah{Measurements} AnonSynth's O-leg netlists pass on 12 of the 15. The other 3 (\code{raygentop}, \code{boundtop}, \code{stereovision0}) are untested, not AnonSynth bugs. The same check fails the Parmys netlist on 8 of the 15, one of them through the lost borrow bit of Sec.~\ref{sec:parmys-bugs}.
\ah{Interpretation} Where the check can run, AnonSynth always matches the RTL, and Parmys does not on 8 of the 15.
\ah{Conclusion} Function is preserved on 12 of 15 designs, with 3 untested. Verdict: USEFUL.

\argid{O7}
\ah{Hypothesis} Rerunning the exit flow gives byte-identical netlists.
\ah{Implication} If true, a result can be reproduced and a difference between two runs has a cause that is not chance.
\ah{Experiment} The exit flow rerun on the 15 designs.
\ah{Methodology} Byte equality of the written netlist.
\ah{Measurements} 15 of 15 are byte-identical.
\ah{Interpretation} Nothing in the flow depends on time, memory addresses or hash order.
\ah{Conclusion} The flow is deterministic on these designs. Verdict: USEFUL.

\argid{O8}
\ah{Hypothesis} The SystemVerilog front end and the cmos6 gate flow run the VerilogEval references.
\ah{Implication} If true, the front end handles a corpus of designs that it was not built around, so it can serve other tools that need such a corpus.
\ah{Experiment} A corpus test that pins all 156 references through the front end and the gate flow.
\ah{Methodology} A reference passes when it elaborates; a refusal must carry a pinned reason. A 20-design sample is compared with Yosys's techmapped output through ABC's combinational equivalence check.
\ah{Measurements} 146 elaborate; 10 are refused with a pinned reason (two explicit casts and eight inferred latches, which the cmos6 library has no cell for). All 20 sampled designs are equivalent.
\ah{Interpretation} About 94\% (146 of 156) of the references run, and each refusal is understood.
\ah{Conclusion} The corpus runs, with ten known refusals. Verdict: USEFUL.

\subsection{Equivalence on the 14 small VTR designs (O5)}

\argid{O5}
\ah{Hypothesis} AnonSynth's netlists are equivalent to Parmys's on the 14 small VTR designs.
\ah{Implication} If true, the front end is a drop-in replacement on real designs, not only on micros.
\ah{Experiment} \code{equiv-check} on the 14 designs, at the Phase~2 exit and after \code{lower}.
\ah{Methodology} A SAT proof of equivalence; a design past the size limit of the solver is unproven, not failed.
\ah{Measurements} At the Phase~2 exit 9 of the 14 were proved equivalent and 5 were untested at the size limit. After \code{lower}, 8 are proven equivalent, 1 is empty, 5 are unproven and none differs.
\ah{Interpretation} The proof found no counterexample, but for 5 of 14 designs it cannot say.
\ah{Conclusion} The numbers do not decide the question. Verdict: INCONCLUSIVE.

\subsection{Defects found in the Parmys flow (O9)}
\label{sec:parmys-bugs}

\argid{O9}
\ah{Hypothesis} Checking against oracles finds defects in the Parmys flow.
\ah{Implication} If true, the oracle method does more than confirm our work: it finds mistakes in the references we compare against, which the open-source projects can fix.
\ah{Experiment} Probes, \code{fcheck} and \code{miter}/\code{sat} counterexamples wherever AnonSynth and Parmys disagree.
\ah{Methodology} Where the two disagree, a probe or an equivalence check decides which matches the RTL; AnonSynth follows the RTL and reports the divergence.
\ah{Measurements} Six defects, each shown by a probe or a counterexample: a lost borrow bit in an unsigned \code{\$sub} chain, a wrong output select in a depth-split RAM, a dropped registered read in the soft memory path, a sign-extended low product when a multiplier is split on operand B, \code{ERROR: BUF\_NODE} on \code{k4\_N4\_90nm}, and a \code{casez} value lost by Yosys~0.55 \code{proc\_mux}. A seventh, D1, where Parmys pads signed adder operands with \code{unconn} instead of sign-extending them, is declared, and we ruled it RTL-correct for AnonSynth. The comparison also found defects in AnonSynth: the lost memories of Sec.~\ref{sec:q-qor} surfaced because the QoR table disagreed with the oracle.
\ah{Interpretation} Six mistakes in a production flow were found by comparison alone, and the comparison also caught our own mistakes. We will report these to the VTR and Yosys projects in good faith.
\ah{Conclusion} Oracles find defects in both directions. Verdict: USEFUL.

\subsection{Provenance at every granularity (O14, O15)}
\label{sec:q-prov}

\argid{O14}
\ah{Hypothesis} Provenance records the construct chain and the joins correctly, and leaves every writer's output unchanged.
\ah{Implication} If true, the property that the dataset rests on is right, and turning provenance on costs no change in any netlist a downstream tool reads.
\ah{Experiment} Three checks: the ctest \code{construct\_golden} compares the construct table dump of each design of \code{tests/loc/constructs} with its stored golden file; the unit test \code{test\_prov\_label} checks joins; the ctest \code{construct\_bytes} and a sweep of 539 micro designs compare every writer output under the four \code{--prov-ast} modes against a build of main.
\ah{Methodology} Golden-file equality for the chain; two expressions of one statement are no join and two blocks are a join, with parents in key order; byte equality of writer outputs.
\ah{Measurements} 541 ctest cases pass, 0 fail (2 skip), among them the three checks; all lint steps pass; the writer outputs are byte-identical across the modes on 539 micro designs (8,927 files per side).
\ah{Interpretation} The chain and joins match the goldens and the unit checks, and no writer output changes in about 9,000 files.
\ah{Conclusion} Provenance is recorded correctly and costs no output change. Verdict: USEFUL.

\argid{O15}
\ah{Hypothesis} The name map plus the netlist can be read back to the same netlist.
\ah{Implication} If true, a tool that has only the exported files, with no AnonSynth running, can recover each object's source.
\ah{Experiment} A write and read-back of each export on 68 micro designs.
\ah{Methodology} Idempotence of BLIF with the map, \code{read\_json} for JSON and \code{read\_verilog -netlist; proc} for Verilog (\code{docs/user/provenance.md}).
\ah{Measurements} BLIF with the map is idempotent on 68 of 68, JSON on 68 of 68 and Verilog on 68 of 68.
\ah{Interpretation} All three exports round trip on the micros; the check does not cover the large designs.
\ah{Conclusion} The exports are faithful on the micros. Verdict: USEFUL.

\subsection{The cost of recording constructs (O16)}
\label{sec:q-cost}

\argid{O16}
\ah{Hypothesis} Recording the construct chain at elaboration (\code{--prov-ast refs}) costs at most 5\% of read time and 10\,MB of memory on large designs.
\ah{Implication} If true, the full chain could be on by default for every user.
\ah{Experiment} Five large designs (\code{LU32PEEng}, \code{LU64PEEng}, \code{arm\_core}, \code{mcml} and \code{mkDelayWorker32B}), seven alternating runs per mode.
\ah{Methodology} \code{read\_verilog} time and peak memory; the bound was set in advance and not tuned.
\ah{Measurements} The time ratios are 1.037 (\code{LU32PEEng}), 0.998 (\code{LU64PEEng}), 1.067 (\code{arm\_core}), 1.029 (\code{mcml}) and 1.054 (\code{mkDelayWorker32B}); the memory difference is under 1\,MB on all five. Two designs exceed 5\%, by 1.7 and 0.4 percentage points: the extra read time is 2.7\,ms on \code{arm\_core} (39.1 to 41.8\,ms) and 1.8\,ms on \code{mkDelayWorker32B} (33.7 to 35.5\,ms), against run-to-run standard deviations of 1.5 and 1.2\,ms in the off mode.
\ah{Interpretation} The memory bound holds. The time bound fails on two of five designs by a margin close to the noise, but it was fixed in advance, so it is a failure.
\ah{Conclusion} The command-line default is therefore \code{off}; the Python API and \code{anonsynth-flow --prov} use \code{refs}. Verdict: DISMISSED.

\subsection{Timing attribution to HDL (O17)}
\label{sec:q-timing}

\argid{O17}
\ah{Hypothesis} Joining VPR's timing report to the HDL does not change VPR's result.
\ah{Implication} If true, the critical path of a design can be traced to its source lines at no cost to the result being traced.
\ah{Experiment} A report-only run with and without the extra report on three designs, and an unmatched-atom check on six.
\ah{Methodology} Identity of the \code{.net}, \code{.place} and \code{.route} files and of the critical-path delay; the count of atoms that the join cannot match.
\ah{Measurements} The three files and the delay are identical on the three designs; no atom is unmatched on the six.
\ah{Interpretation} The attribution adds information and changes nothing on these designs; three designs are few.
\ah{Conclusion} The attribution is neutral on three designs. Verdict: POSSIBLY GOOD, narrow.

\section{Discussion and Limits}
\label{sec:discussion}

Self-evolved ABC~\cite{yu2026selfevolvedabc} and VPR-Evolve~\cite{wu2026vprevolve} let agents rewrite tools that already exist, with a benchmark score as the judge. AnonSynth had no code base to start from, so the specification came first and the judge was equivalence against oracles. Three observations follow. An existing tool becomes a specification that can be executed: the oracles let us check work that no human had read, and they found defects in the oracles themselves. Human time shifted from writing code to writing specifications, making decisions, and choosing what to test. And the limit was the machine, not the agents.

\emph{What was not confirmed.} O1: two designs differ from Parmys in multiplier count and five lose adders. O3: per-design parity with Parmys was retired by decision, not reached. O5: equivalence stops at five designs past the size limit of the proof. O16: recording constructs at elaboration costs more than 5\% on two of five large designs, so provenance with constructs is opt-in on the command line.

What this means for the field: the ``superpower'' of building complex software with agents is liberating for an experienced engineer, and for an inexperienced one it is an educational question, whether it builds or limits the engineer's semantic knowledge of synthesis. We would develop that knowledge through first-principles exercises and System Thinking~\cite{cabrera2015systems}. What experiments we can now run is limited more by our imagination and time than by having to build the tool.

\section{Future Work}
\label{sec:future}

Five hypotheses are untested; each is the idea and the experiment it needs. The cost of provenance names in VPR: the Oprov leg of the QoR harness, the same 15 designs through VPR with the names and without, comparing the four result metrics and the run time. The five largest VTR designs (\code{mcml}, \code{LU32PEEng}, \code{bgm}, \code{LU8PEEng} and \code{LU64PEEng}): legs P and O$'$ on a machine with more memory, with the same gate. Latches with reset and the \code{\$\_DFFSR\_*} cell types: the refinement proof and the functional check on added micros. The plain-LUT architecture on \code{mkDelayWorker32B}: a rerun with a limit longer than the 3,600\,s that stopped it. Near-critical paths attributed to HDL objects: the v1.1.1 timing attribution with a window, reporting per HDL construct the share of near-critical paths over the 15 designs, with VPR's result checked unchanged.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

AnonSynth answers the final-frontier question with a qualified yes. A Dark Factory, watched daily by a human and checked against Yosys, Parmys and Odin~II as oracles, built a Verilog front end that keeps Odin~II's good ideas, follows Yosys's semantics, and plugs into the VTR flow: all 15 VTR designs that fit the development machine route, with geomean results inside the gate against Parmys. It adds what neither predecessor has: provenance from every netlist object back to its HDL (off by default on the command line, because of the cost in O16) and a versioned C ABI for plugins and AI tools (stated here, not tested). Of the hypotheses tested, nine are confirmed, four possibly good, one failed and two inconclusive; five are untested. No one has read the code deeply, and we've tested it on a subset of scenarios; the oracles make more testing possible. The experiments we could not run with Yosys come next, in AnonGUI and AnonRE, which use AnonSynth as a package (URL withheld for review).
