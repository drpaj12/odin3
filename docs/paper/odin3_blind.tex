\begin{abstract}
Odin was written in 2005 and Odin~II in 2010. Odin~II became the Verilog front end of the VTR FPGA CAD flow, and Yosys with the Parmys plugin has since displaced it. After the ``MAIking software'' moment of December 2025, when AI coding agents began to make real software, and after the first agent-evolved EDA tools, I asked the final-frontier question: could a Dark Factory, in which agents turn a human's specification into software, build AnonSynth, matching Odin~II's good ideas and plugging into the VTR flow? This paper tells that story and reports what was built and how it was tested. AnonSynth (MIT license) is a C17 framework with one provenance-tracked IR, two-stage elaboration, re-implementations of Yosys~0.55's \code{proc}, \code{opt}, \code{fsm}, \code{wreduce} and \code{flatten}, architecture-driven partial mapping, linked ABC and VPR-ready BLIF, and, in v1.1.0, a SystemVerilog front end over the slang library whose gate flow runs 144 of the 156 VerilogEval references, behind a versioned C ABI for plugins and AI tools. Every netlist it produced for the 15 VTR benchmarks that fit the development laptop routes in VPR, with geomean CLB, channel-width, delay and wirelength ratios against the Parmys flow inside a 1.20 gate. Checking against oracles also found defects in the Parmys flow. I state the caveats of this MAIking: a human watched every day, the code has not been deeply read by a human, and testing covers a subset of scenarios. I also report the time and token cost and what the process suggests for CAD research.  Finally, we present the AnonSuite ecosystem, now made possible by this approach.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Odin~\cite{jamieson2005odin} started from nothing. It used Icarus Verilog~\cite{icarus} as the front end, but Icarus treats Verilog as a simulation language, and building synthesis on a simulator's front end cost a year of work to learn the basics of Verilog synthesis. Odin~II~\cite{jamieson2011odin2} followed and wrote a proper compiler front end: a Bison and Flex parser, and elaboration into a flattened netlist. Odin~II became the Verilog front end of the VTR flow~\cite{rose2012vtr,luu2014vtr7}, and it mapped multipliers, memories, and adders onto the hard blocks that an architecture file describes. VTR has since moved its default front end to Parmys~\cite{parmys}, a plugin for Yosys~\cite{wolf2013yosys,yosys} that carries Odin~II's partial mapping, and the VTR documentation now marks Odin~II as deprecated.

Then came what I call the ``MAIking software'' moment of December 2025: AI coding agents~\cite{claudecode} became able to make real software. Yu and Ren's multi-agent self-evolved ABC~\cite{yu2026selfevolvedabc} showed that LLM agents can change the source of a million-line logic synthesis system, rebuild it, check it, and keep the changes that improve its results; VPR-Evolve~\cite{wu2026vprevolve} has since done the same for FPGA place and route, and Ding et al.\ survey high-level synthesis in the agentic era~\cite{ding2026hlsagentic}. Those works build on existing tools, and together they raised the final-frontier question I had to ask. Dan Shapiro describes five levels of AI-assisted programming; at the last, the \emph{Dark Factory}, the software process is ``a black box that turns specs into software''~\cite{shapiro2026darkfactory}. Could I use a Dark Factory to build AnonSynth, a front end that matches Odin~II's good ideas and plugs into the VTR flow where Yosys has displaced Odin~II? This paper describes the answer to that question (YES!), what has been built to answer it, and how it is tested. AnonSynth is public (URL withheld for review).

The contributions of this work are:
\begin{itemize}
\item an account of building a Yosys-class FPGA synthesis front end with a Dark Factory under daily human oversight, specification first and checked against oracles, with its time and token cost (Sec.~\ref{sec:results});
\item the architecture: one provenance-tracked IR with word and bit views, two-stage elaboration, architecture-driven partial mapping, linked ABC and VPR-ready BLIF, and a versioned C ABI for plugins and AI tools (Sec.~\ref{sec:arch});
\item the evidence: quality of results against the Parmys flow on the VTR benchmarks that fit the development machine, equivalence and functional checks, byte-identical reruns, and defects in the Parmys flow found against oracles, to be reported upstream (Secs.~\ref{sec:results}, \ref{sec:parmys-bugs});
\item a presentation of how we created the overarching AnonSuite, and a description of what it is, including a reimplementation of an earlier Verilog teaching game~\cite{anon01}, now called AnonGame.
\item a discussion of what the process suggests for CAD research (Sec.~\ref{sec:discussion}).
\end{itemize}

The approach has limits, and I state them first. It was not a lights-out factory. I watched the agents build and interacted with them daily: I wrote the design specification and goals, ruled on every design rule (architecture decisions, IR invariants, mapping algorithms), and managed the risks of a small machine. Everything ran on one Windows desktop (Intel i7-13700) inside a WSL2 virtual machine with 12 logical processors and 23\, GiB of memory, where an out-of-memory condition or a full host disk kills the virtual machine and every agent in it. Heavy jobs therefore run one at a time under a machine-wide lock in a memory-capped slice, and the largest VTR designs were shipped out to a bigger machine instead of being run. The codebase has not been deeply read by a human. It took 14 calendar days to reach version~1.0 (first commit 2026-10-08, v1.0.0 released 2026-10-21, both days counted) and was tested with a subset of the possible scenarios. Odin~II and Yosys with Parmys are production oracles, so more testing is always possible. We found defects in the Parmys flow (Sec.~\ref{sec:parmys-bugs}), which we will report to those projects in good faith, because we want to contribute to these open-source CAD tools.

The community may object to such a process. It may also ask a harder question: if a guided Dark Factory with oracles can rebuild a tool like this, could a reverse-engineering process let open-source tools rip out the human-years of engineering behind today's EDA tool landscape? (To the companies, I would suggest moving to a cloud service model as soon as possible.) My goal is narrower: to run experiments I couldn't do with Yosys, and with AnonSynth I can now, and the ideas I hoped my students could pursue were limited by inflexibilities in the toolflow.

I call this new suite AnonSuite (Fig.~\ref{fig:yggdrasil}). AnonSynth is the trunk: its steps run from the roots (Verilog and netlists in) to the crown (BLIF for VTR out), and every path passes through its one provenance IR, which is why I see it as a final frontier. The oracles (Yosys, Parmys, Odin~II, VTR's golden results, and ABC) stand as a small forest beside the trunk, to compare against. Three deep roots reach three wells, the outside work the suite draws on: prior work and benchmarks, FPGA CAD and its views, and synthesis sources; the oracle trees drink from the third. In the canopy are AnonGUI (the experiment runner and GUI); AnonSim (the simulation bridge); and AnonGame, a port of an earlier Verilog teaching game~\cite{anon02}, which AnonGUI connects to. AnonData, the circuit knowledge base, hangs from the canopy as the fruit: the dataset harvested from the runs of the trunk and the branches. Four tools and experiments grow on side branches off the trunk. AnonRE (reverse engineering) continues earlier netlist-to-RTL work~\cite{anon03} with an LLM-free L0-L2 flow (provenance datasets, a Bayesian signal-role classifier, clustering and graph-network reconstruction), now measured against the baseline's own metrics; AnonHT (hardware Trojan detection tools); AnonPlace, an earlier placement experiment~\cite{anon04,anon05}, redone with provenance (Sec.~\ref{sec:provenance-problem}); and AnonTrace~III (the Micro through the Macro), an interactive record-and-replay walk of a micro design through a real AnonSynth run. All the code is public (URL withheld for review).

\section{Background}
\label{sec:background}

\subsection{Two open-source models of Verilog synthesis}

\emph{The Odin~II flow.} Odin~II~\cite{jamieson2011odin2} parses Verilog with Bison and Flex into an abstract syntax tree (AST); passes over the AST and the module hierarchy turn it into one flattened netlist of nodes, pins, and nets. It reads the VPR architecture file to decide which multipliers, adders, and memories become hard blocks, writes the rest as BLIF for ABC~\cite{brayton2010abc} to map to LUTs, and includes a netlist simulator.

\emph{The Yosys approach.} Yosys~\cite{wolf2013yosys} parses Verilog into an AST, and \code{genrtlil} turns each module into RTLIL, a word-level netlist of wires, cells, processes and memories, with the hierarchy kept. From then on, every step is a pass over RTLIL that never sees the AST: \code{proc}, the \code{opt} family, \code{flatten}, \code{techmap} and \code{abc}. Yosys is the reference open synthesis suite, with a large pass library and SystemVerilog and VHDL through plugins~\cite{yosysslang,ghdl}.

\subsection{VTR and partial mapping}

Both models attach to VTR~\cite{luu2009vpr5,rose2012vtr,luu2014vtr7,murray2020vtr8}: a front end, ABC, then VPR's packing, placement, and routing on an XML-described architecture. Jason Luu's work brought in partial mapping. His architecture description language~\cite{luu2011packing} describes logic blocks with hierarchy, modes, and complex interconnects, and models each hard block's primitive, so a front end that reads the same file can map part of a design onto hard blocks and leave the rest soft for ABC. VTR~7.0~\cite{luu2014vtr7} and the study of hard adders and carry chains~\cite{luu2014hardadders} carried this to adders, with front-end optimizations that ease the restrictions hardened arithmetic places on logic synthesis. Parmys~\cite{parmys} brings the same partial mapping onto Yosys's word-level netlists, runs inside VTR's pinned Yosys~0.55, and is now VTR's default front end.

\subsection{The provenance problem}
\label{sec:provenance-problem}

Synthesis modifies some of the designer's functional intention. Flattening removes the hierarchy, optimization merges and splits operators, and LUT mapping covers the logic with cells that have no source line names. I call the task of linking each object of the final netlist back to the HDL that produced it the \emph{provenance problem}; it is one of the major pieces of this work going forward. Earlier work addressed it in placement: simulated annealing produces a flattened design that loses its HDL hierarchy, so they recovered system-level information from the flat netlist and analyzed its correlation with locality in FPGA placement~\cite{anon04,anon05}, then identified and placed clusters found in the placement data~\cite{anon06}. Later work applies data-mining clustering to such combinatorial optimization algorithms~\cite{anon07}. Goeders and Wilton address the same problem in in-system debug: signals in a high-level synthesis-built circuit must be traced back to their source, and they developed signal-tracing and debug techniques for such circuits on FPGAs~\cite{goeders2014effective,goeders2015dynamic,goeders2017signaltracing}. Each line of work had to recover a link the synthesis tool didn't keep. AnonSynth keeps it (Sec.~\ref{sec:arch}), so its analysis flow (provenance coverage, \code{levelize}, graph export) can redo the placement-correlation experiment with the link to the HDL measured rather than recovered.

\subsection{Oracles, ASTs and elaboration}

In this project, an oracle is a trusted reference tool whose output we compare against: Yosys with Parmys, and Odin~II. \code{tools/run-oracle.sh} runs an oracle on a design and an architecture and stores its netlist, logs, and commit as a \emph{golden}; AnonSynth's output is compared with the golden for identity after canonical renaming and for functional equivalence. An oracle is trusted because it is the reference, not because it is always right (Sec.~\ref{sec:parmys-bugs}). Odin~II transforms the AST into one flat netlist, while Yosys builds each module separately and flattens later as a pass on the graph; AnonSynth takes the RTLIL route with its own split (Sec.~\ref{sec:arch}).  We note that an oracle makes the process much simpler than the original creation of Odin and Odin~II, which humans verified at high cost.

\section{Architecture}
\label{sec:arch}

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centering
\odinfit{\textwidth}{\input{IMAGE/blind/fig_flow}}
\caption{The flow of a Verilog design through AnonSynth, in the order of the production script. Each box is a stage; the text under it says what it does. Every stage from \code{proc} to \code{write\_blif} is a registered pass.}
\label{fig:flow}
\end{figure*}

AnonSynth reads a design into one IR and changes it one pass at a time. Figure~\ref{fig:flow} shows the path of a Verilog design through the stages, in the order of the production script:
\emph{One IR, two views.} Each cell type carries a granularity tag (\code{word}, \code{bit}, \code{hard}, \code{blackbox}); the word-level (RTLIL) and bit-level (netlist) views are assertions over that tag, so every reader, writer, checker, and the simulator exist once. Every net is one bit, ports are nodes, constants are cells, and every reference is a typed 32-bit ID into per-module paged storage, never a pointer. Every reader, writer, and transformation is a pass, and in Debug builds the pass manager runs a structural \code{check} before and after each.

\emph{Elaboration in two stages.} A project record names the files, the top, the parameters and, unusually for a front end, the architecture and the mapping rules. A preprocessor and an LALR(1) parser build an AST with source locations. Stage~1 is Verilog-specific: it resolves parameters, \code{generate} and constant functions into an \emph{elaborated AST}. Stage~2 is language-neutral: it binds names, sizes expressions, and builds word-level cells, keeping each \code{always} block as a \code{\$proc} cell. The seam lets a later front end enter at stage~2.

\emph{Word-level passes.} \code{proc} lowers \code{\$proc} cells to multiplexer trees, latches and flip-flops in Yosys~0.55's order, and \code{fsm} recodes state machines where Parmys does. \code{opt} is Yosys's family of rewrites, \code{flatten} inlines instances top-down keeping each object's instance path, and \code{wreduce} narrows widths, which decides hard-block counts. Every walk that Yosys writes recursively is an explicit worklist here, and every input-driven quantity has a named cap, so a hostile input gives a located error, not a crash.

\emph{Partial mapping.} \code{partial\_map} (script name \code{hardmap}) binds word-level multipliers, adders, and RAMs to the hard blocks that the architecture lists and leaves the rest soft. Parmys's rule is the default; \code{read\_arch} fills a tech library, and an arch record from a VPR architecture file, and generic hard blocks match by exact port signature, as in Odin~II.

\emph{Bits, LUTs and BLIF.} \code{lower} turns every remaining word cell into bit cells. \code{abc} runs a linked ABC once per clock domain and returns LUTs as one registry cell, \code{\$lut}, with VTR's own script as the default (other scripts are allow-listed and run in a child process). \code{write\_blif --vtr} writes the top module as BLIF that VPR reads.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\odinfit{\columnwidth}{\input{IMAGE/blind/fig_prov}}
\caption{The provenance lineage DAG (illustrative run and operation numbers). Two \code{\$add}s from one line of a generate loop get two SOURCE records; lowering each is one operation with one DERIVED record; clumping two gates is one operation with two parents.}
\label{fig:prov}
\end{figure}

\emph{Provenance.} Every node, net and wire carries one 32-bit ID into an append-only lineage DAG (Fig.~\ref{fig:prov}). A record is SOURCE (made from the HDL), IMPORTED (read from a netlist) or DERIVED (made by a pass, with its parents), and it holds the pass run and the operation within it, so 100 adders from one line of a generate loop stay distinguishable. The walk runs backward to the source line and forward from \code{file:line}, and the writers can name each object \code{hier/path/name@file:line}, so a VPR block traces to its source line without AnonSynth loaded. This is AnonSynth's answer to the provenance problem (Sec.~\ref{sec:provenance-problem}).

\subsection{Linking into VTR: a proof of steps}
\label{sec:vtr-link}

Plugging into VTR is not the real goal of AnonSynth; it is the proof that each step works on real designs. \code{write\_blif --vtr} refuses, before any byte is written, every construct VPR would reject, and \code{blif-vtr-check} mirrors VPR's BLIF checks so that CI gets VPR's verdicts without VTR. The QoR harness \code{vtr-qor} runs VTR's own scripts, read-only, in \emph{legs} that separate the causes of a gap: \textbf{P} is the Parmys flow (the baseline), \textbf{O} is AnonSynth alone with its linked ABC, then VPR, and \textbf{O$'$} is AnonSynth's netlist through VTR's own ABC stage. Its gate is a geometric mean (geomean) ratio of at most 1.20, and no design above 1.50, on CLB count, channel width, critical-path delay, and routed wirelength. Where AnonSynth and Parmys could differ, \code{--vtr-compat} makes AnonSynth do what Parmys does and print a WARNING; where Parmys is wrong to the RTL, AnonSynth stays correct and declares the difference (Sec.~\ref{sec:parmys-bugs}).

\subsection{The ABI: extensions and AI tools}
\label{sec:abi-summary}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\odinfit{\columnwidth}{\input{IMAGE/blind/fig_abi}}
\caption{The C ABI and plugin model. Everything outside the core sees only \code{anonsynth.h}.}
\label{fig:abi}
\end{figure}

An application binary interface (ABI) is the contract between compiled code: function names, types, structure layouts, and calling conventions. A plugin built against one version of AnonSynth must load against a later build without a recompile, and a C ABI helps: no name mangling, no class layouts, no exceptions across the boundary, and other languages can call it. \code{include/anonsynth/anonsynth.h} is the only header a plugin sees (Fig.~\ref{fig:abi}). ABI version~4 exposes an opaque design handle and raw 32-bit IDs; every function returns a status; \code{libanonsynth.so} exports only what the header declares (a script checks it), and a plugin that asks for another ABI version is refused. Plugins register passes and cell types from an \code{anonsynth\_plugin\_init} entry point, and Python uses the same header through cffi.

The same boundary is how AI tools attach. Agents built AnonSynth by driving its command-line scripts and reading its tools' exit codes, which follow one convention (0 holds, 1 differs, 2 tool error, 3 hard failure, 4 unproven, 77 skipped), so a controller can act on a result without parsing text. Through the Python binding, an agent, or a learned model, can walk the IR and its provenance and add cell types for the structures it recognizes, recording its own decisions as DERIVED provenance records. The analysis flow of v1.0 includes a graph export (Yosys JSON plus versioned AnonSynth keys) that networkx and PyTorch Geometric load without AnonSynth. A tool-calling server for language models would be one more plugin over this ABI; none is built yet.  This boundary and the emerging AI experiments that researchers would like to do hinge on this point.

We left out the largest VTR~7.0 designs (\code{mcml}, \code{LU32PEEng}, \code{bgm}, \code{LU8PEEng} and \code{LU64PEEng}) because of the weakness of my development machine; we claim nothing for them here.

Table~\ref{tab:m2} gives the M2 run: AnonSynth's netlist of every one of the 15 designs routed in VPR. The geomean ratios against Parmys are 1.041 for LUTs, 1.012 for CLBs, 1.000 for memories, 0.985 for minimum channel width, 0.986 for critical-path delay, and 1.034 for routed wirelength, inside the 1.20 gate, and no design exceeds the per-design bound of 1.50 on a gated metric. The one LUT outlier is \code{blob\_merge} (1.78$\times$), whose CLB ratio is only 1.04; \code{raygentop}, at 1.42$\times$ in the earlier run, is now 1.03. An earlier run showed \code{mkPktMerge} and \code{boundtop} packing no memory blocks, a real bug found by the oracle comparison: VTR's ABC step lost RAMs whose outputs drove primary outputs directly, and plain \code{opt} turned \code{x} multiplexer data into constants. The fix adds a port buffer and uses Parmys's \code{opt -full} after \code{flatten}, and \code{vtr-qor} now flags any lost hard block. Both designs pack all their memories (15 and 1, equal to Parmys). With its linked ABC (leg O), AnonSynth also routes \code{arm\_core} in 148\,s. No O-leg QoR table was run for v1.0: Phase~3 closed on the existing M2 and M3 data, because the front end to elaboration is the priority (PHASE3 decision 58).

Each stage is checked by a tool separate from the code, against an oracle, under the exit-code convention of Sec.~\ref{sec:abi-summary}, in which a list of expected differences never turns an unproven result into a pass. Figure~\ref{fig:flow-verify} marks which technique checks which stage.

\emph{Equivalence on micros.} The \code{lower} refinement proof (\code{lower-equiv}), run on the micro-benchmarks, gives 399 ok, 140 expected and 0 unexpected results, with 272 designs compared; a 526-fixture sweep compared 518 and found no difference, the other 8 being unproven at the SAT size limit. A random generator of Verilog modules, checked against Yosys~0.55's \code{techmap}, gives 200 ok of 200 seeds; a 5,000-seed run gave 4,830 ok, 142 structural differences that are proved equivalent, 28 empty designs and no bug.

\emph{Equivalence on 14 small VTR designs.} At the Phase~2 exit, 9 of the 14 were proved equivalent to Parmys and 5 were untested at the SAT size limit. After \code{lower}, 8 are proven equivalent, 1 is empty, 5 are unproven, and none differs.

\emph{Functional check against the source.} \code{fcheck} runs VTR's own random check, which builds the RTL and the netlist with Verilator and drives both with 50,000 random vectors. AnonSynth's O-leg netlists pass on 12 of the 15 designs; the other 3 (\code{raygentop}, \code{boundtop}, \code{stereovision0}) are untested, not AnonSynth bugs. The same check fails the Parmys netlist on 8 of the 15, among them through the defect D2b below.

\emph{Determinism.} Rerunning the exit flow gives byte-identical netlists on 15 of 15 designs.

\emph{The VerilogEval corpus.} v1.1.0 pins all 156 VerilogEval references in a corpus test: 144 elaborate through the SystemVerilog front end and the cmos6 gate flow, and 12 are refused with a pinned reason (two explicit casts, eight inferred latches, which the cmos6 library has no cell for, and two compound bit-select assignments). A 20-design equivalence sample compares the gated netlists with Yosys's techmapped output through ABC's combinational equivalence check; all 20 are equivalent.

\section{Discussion: What the Process Suggests for CAD Research}
\label{sec:discussion}

Self-evolved ABC~\cite{yu2026selfevolvedabc} and VPR-Evolve~\cite{wu2026vprevolve} let agents rewrite tools that already exist, with a benchmark score as the judge, and Ding et al.~\cite{ding2026hlsagentic} ask what agents change for high-level synthesis. AnonSynth had no code base to start from, so the specification came first, was reviewed by a human, and the judge checked equivalence against oracles before any quality number counted. Three observations follow. First, an existing tool becomes a specification that can be executed: the oracles made it possible to check work that no human had read, and they found defects in the oracles themselves. Second, human time shifted from writing code to writing specifications, making decisions, and choosing what to test. Third, the limit was the machine, not the agents: memory caps, a lock for heavy jobs, and shipped-out designs shaped the method as much as any design decision.

What implications does this work push to our field:
\begin{itemize}
\item{I believe the ``superpower'' as I call the creation of complex software is both liberating and concerning.  For an experienced engineer like myself who has the burden of ``job'' this superpower has allowed me to MAIke again.  For the inexperienced engineer, the question is whether it is a superpower or a crutch that will limit the individual.  This educational question, I will argue, is the crux of our time.}
\item{To the crux, I believe the path forward is developing an engineer's semantic knowledge. What does a builder need to know about synthesis that the agents
could not supply, and how did that knowledge enter the specification?  Many claim this is a critical thinking problem.  I'm not happy with how we define that term; instead, I will say it is developing System Thinking~\cite{cabrera2015systems} through first-principles exercises (humans building and testing the system) and determining the right moments for second-principle design (the Making).  This too is a research question we need answered - when?}
\item{The final frontier. What this means for CAD research, for open-source tools and for the EDA industry?  If anything, I see the possibilities that grow deeply with this ``superpower''.  What experiments I can now run is limited more by my imagination (and time) than by the fact that I have to build the tool.}
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

AnonSynth answers the final-frontier question with a qualified yes. A Dark Factory, watched daily by an experienced FPGA CAD developer and checked against Yosys, Parmys and Odin~II as oracles, built a Verilog front end that keeps Odin~II's good ideas (the node, pin and net model, the architecture file and partial mapping), follows Yosys's semantics, and plugs into the VTR flow: all 15 VTR designs that fit the development machine route, with geomean results inside the gate against Parmys. It adds what neither predecessor has: provenance from every netlist object back to its HDL, and a versioned C ABI through which plugins and AI tools extend it. No one has read the code deeply, and we've tested it on a subset of scenarios; the oracles make more testing possible. The experiments I could not run with Yosys come next, in AnonGUI (a GUI and experiment runner) and AnonRE (reverse engineering), which use AnonSynth as a package (URL withheld for review).
